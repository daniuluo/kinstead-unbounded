# Kinstead

A little isometric village builder that runs in your browser — pixel-art rendering in the spirit of *Metropolis 1998* and the late-90s city sims, with a living village of little minions going about their day.

**Play it:** https://daniuluo.github.io/kinstead/play/ · **Wiki:** https://daniuluo.github.io/kinstead/wiki/ · **Home page:** https://daniuluo.github.io/kinstead/ (the live site plays the main game; this fork runs from a copy of its own: see [Running locally](#running-locally))

## This fork: Kinstead without the grid

This branch (`gridless`) is Kinstead with the tile grid taken out. The buildings, villagers, research, money, transit and multiplayer are the same game; what changes is how a town is laid out on the ground:

- **Buildings anywhere, at any angle.** A plot is a rectangle (a unit square for a cottage, 2 × 1 for a terrace, 2 × 2 for a town hall, and so on, as before) that stands wherever it is put and turns by itself to face the nearest street, square to it, however that street runs. `Z` / `C` turn it further from there (15° a press, 5° with `Shift`, or `Alt` / `Ctrl` and the wheel), and `Tab` lines it up along the street instead, its front the right distance from the road. A building needs the middle of its front within reach of a road (or of either half of its front, for a wide one; a park needs none), and its plot may not overlap another, a road, track, fields or water. One already standing can be turned in its panel, or moved to a new spot at a new angle. Past the end of a road a building faces the end itself.
- **Roads are curves.** Drag the road tool and the street follows the stroke as a smooth curve (hold `Shift` for a straight one). Where it crosses a road it makes a crossroads, where it meets one a junction, and a street begun at a road's end carries on from it. A bend too tight to drive, a road run too close alongside another and one meeting another at too shallow an angle are refused, saying so. The roads are a network of stretches between junctions and ends ([`src/world/net.js`](src/world/net.js)); the surface is drawn from one distance field of the stretches, so junctions and bends are rounded and kerbed whatever their angle ([`src/world/streets.js`](src/world/streets.js)). A click picks a stretch; its panel upgrades or downgrades it, dresses it (an avenue, hedges, lamps, or a planted median on a paved street), buries its power lines or takes it up, all priced by its length. Track is laid the same way, and trains, coaches, carts and villagers follow the curves, each keeping to its side of the road.
- **Neighbours side by side.** Joining two buildings, a big home buying a strip of a cottage's garden, and taking the land beside or behind a building work between plots standing square to each other and sharing a side, as the town's own builders lay them out along a street (and as `Tab` lines them up). The 2 × 2 estates and the bigger grounds of parks and the seat of government grow onto the land beside a plot in its own frame, at whatever angle it stands.
- **Fields are squares of farmland** a unit across, at the farm's own angle (or turned one of their own), touching the farm or its other fields, behind and beside it.
- **No diagonal streets, boulevards or nudges on a plot**: a curve already runs any way, and a building already turns any way. (A boulevard look offered to a street is refused, saying so.)
- **Distances are in units.** Where this page or the wiki still says tiles, read units: a unit is the width of a cottage's plot, and a valley is still 48 to 96 of them across. The coverage, appeal and pollution maps are still kept a unit square at a time, as the land's heights are.
- **Saves.** A town saved by this fork is a version 2 save: the roads and track as curves, each building with its middle and its angle, the fields as squares. A town saved by the main game (version 1) loads here: each plot becomes a rectangle standing where it stood, facing one of the four ways (turned only where that's what reaches its street, and fits), each run of road squares a curve through their middles, and each field a square of farmland. From then on it saves as version 2, which the main game can't load. Merging villages works as before, curves and all.
- **Multiplayer** is the same, with its protocol number moved on (101), so a guest needs this fork as well.
- **Tests.** The same scripts run here (`check.js`, `starters.js`, `merge-test.js`, `net-check.js`, `playtest.js`, `collide.js`, `site-check.js`), set up with curves and plots along them; `scripts/geom-check.js` tests the geometry underneath, and `check.js` loads a save made by the main game (`scripts/fixtures/grid-town.json`).

## Features

- **Pixel-art isometric 3D** — the scene is rendered in real 3D (three.js), then drawn at low resolution with depth/normal outlines and crisp pixel upscaling, using a classic 2:1 isometric camera (which can also turn and tilt freely, or look through a perspective lens: see *Free camera*). Settings let you choose the pixel size or switch to a smooth, antialiased renderer with ambient occlusion. Both use neutral tone mapping, so the colours stay as painted (the smooth look used to grey them), and the smooth look keeps only a light haze over the far distance.
- **Rotating view** — press `Q` / `E` or the rotate buttons beside `2D` to swing the camera a quarter turn around the ground at the middle of the screen and see the town from all four sides. The sun stays where it is in the sky, so shadows keep falling the same way across the ground and the far side of the town is the shaded side.
- **2D top-down map view** — press `T` or the `2D` button to swing the camera straight down for a grid-aligned map (it rotates too); zoom in and out with the wheel or the `+`/`−` buttons, from the whole valley right down to a single villager, near enough to see their goggles.
- **Free camera** — turn the view to any angle and tilt it from just above the horizon (8°) to straight down: middle-drag (or `Shift` + right-drag) across to turn and up or down to tilt, hold `Shift` with `W` `A` `S` `D` or the arrows, or on a touch screen twist two fingers to turn and drag two fingers side by side up or down to tilt. From a free angle `Q` / `E` go to the next quarter turn, and the 2D map remembers the angle and tilt to come back to. **Settings → Camera** switches to a **perspective** camera (field of view 30°, 45° or 60°) with the same scale at the middle of the screen. `Home`, or the compass button beside the turn buttons (it turns with the view), goes back to the classic isometric view. Picking, zoom toward the pointer, shadows (fitted to the ground in view), the haze, tilt-shift and the pixel-art outlines (worked out from linear depth for the perspective camera) all work at any angle.
- **Procedural valley** — every seed grows a different landscape: rivers, lakes, hills, forests and rocky slopes, shown as a diorama. Pick a size: Small 32×32, Medium 48×48, Large 64×64 or Huge 80×80. Terraform by raising, lowering and levelling land, and plant trees.
- **Highland valleys** — a new village picks how high the land round it stands: **Gentle** (the valleys as they always were), **Hilly** (the default: the valley floor ringed by highland behind steep escarpments, broken by a few broad slopes where a road can climb, with rivers cutting gorges through it, like La Paz in its bowl under El Alto) or **Mountains** (higher still, a second tier further out and peaks on it). The village is always founded on the valley floor; the escarpments are too steep for roads or building but where they ease, so the way up is long on foot and short by gondola ([`RELIEF`](src/world/terrain.js)). Starter towns and saved towns keep their own valleys.
- **Starter towns** — found a village in an empty valley, or take over one that's a few weeks along: a **riverside hamlet** (cottages, a woodcutter and a fishing hut by the river), a **market village** (a cobbled high street with a bakery, tavern, school and family houses), a **mill town** (farms, a workshop, a chapel and a water wheel powering the town), a **crossroads village** (a coaching tavern, a smithy and a coach line out to every corner), a whole **cathedral city** ten years along (about 580 buildings and 3,000 villagers on an 80×80 map), or the **Metropolis**, the most people a valley can hold (about 47,000 in 2,600 buildings: see below). Each card in the new-village window shows a little map of the town, its size, villagers and money, and picking one shows it behind the window.
- **Organic village** — no two buildings look the same, in shape as well as colour. Each one is generated from a seed: walls, roofs (thatch, tile, slate, shingle), half-timbering, chimneys, dormers, shutters, flower boxes, gardens and fences. Paths are worn into the grass and meander a little.
  - **Every building its own shape.** Buildings with the same name and level don't share a shape: each one's seed gives it a form of its own (`formOf` in [`src/render/forms.js`](src/render/forms.js)), kept as it is upgraded, on every load and for every multiplayer peer. Its roofs: gable, hip, half-hip, saltbox, cross gable, mansard, gambrel or a flat roof behind a parapet, as its style allows; a gable to the street, finished plain, crow-stepped, curved or Dutch. Its dormers (gabled, hipped or flat), its porch or door case (a canopy, a gabled porch, a hood, a pediment, a fanlight, a portico, a stoop, a veranda), a bay, an oriel, a balcony, a turret on a corner, and a cupola, finials or a weather vane on the ridge.
  - **Plans.** A cottage is a plain block, a long house with its byre under a lower roof, an L with a cross wing, a dormered cottage, an outshut under a catslide roof or a square cottage under a pyramid roof; a family house a double pile, an L, a narrow house with its gable to the street, a gabled bay in the middle of the front, a square house with a veranda or a house with a low side wing. Villas can be Italianate (a belvedere tower), Gothic (a steep gable over a bay) or Regency (a veranda and a bow window); manors an E, a Palladian front with pavilions, a tower house or a court; grand manors domed, Jacobean or a château. Townhouse flats, tenements, Mansion Blocks (a mansard, domed corner turrets, shaped gables, a pavilion or a stair tower, some a storey taller), terraces (one roof, a gable over every house, a mansard, a Georgian parapet or every house its own height), mansions, apartments and quadrangles vary the same way.
  - **Shops** can stand a storey taller than others of their level, with a shopfront of their own (plain, pilastered with consoles, transom lights, or the door set back in a lobby) and a second sign (a blade sign, a painted band or a board along the eaves). The market stall, the Market Hall (an arcade under a hip, Gothic, classical, iron and glass, or timber on posts), the bank, the counting house, chambers, the office building, the department store, the exchange, the arcade and the inn each have several plans.
  - **Services, civic, farms, industry, transit, utilities and parks.** Chapels set their tower at the front, beside them or over the middle, crowned with a spire, a pyramid, a saddleback, a belfry, battlements or a lantern (a shared `tower`), or carry a bell-cote; minsters have twin towers, one spire or a crossing tower. Schools, watch posts (square or round), fire stations, hospitals, almshouses, colleges and the town hall within its style vary their towers, wings and roofs. Farms build a gable barn, a Dutch barn or a long barn, an oast house, a silo of concrete, timber or brick, and a tower or post mill. Works and yards are laid out either way round (`GeoBuilder.mirrorX`), and the Manufactory, foundry and paper mill have plans of their own. Coach stops have five kinds of shelter, stations three canopies; wells, water towers, waterworks, wind generators, pumps, mills and power stations their own forms; tennis courts and bowling greens turn to run back from the street.
- **Upgradable buildings** across homes, commerce (market, bakery, tavern, and the high street below), industry (farm, woodcutter, fishery, workshop, builder's yard), services (well, clinic, school, watch, chapel, town hall), and parks and decor. Most buildings have three or four levels, up to a Market Hall, Hospital, Academy or Minster. Upgrades need happiness and service coverage, and each option shows what changes (homes, jobs, earnings, reach, upkeep…) before you commit.
- **A high street** — besides the market, bakery and tavern, the Commerce menu has a shop for each trade, unlocked from the first villagers to the late game: a **Greengrocer** (at 25 villagers, no research: produce banked up on trestles under a green-and-white awning) → Fruiterer → Grocery & Provisions; a brick **Butcher's Shop** (Shopkeeping: joints on a rail across the window, a blind rolled up under the fascia, a block on the pavement) → Family Butcher (white-tiled front, marble slab) → Butcher & Poulterer (game and birds along a second rail); a gable-fronted **Cobbler's Shop** (Shopkeeping: a bow window of boots, a boot hung over the door) → Boot & Shoe Shop (a gilded boot); a painted **Tailor's Shop** with dormers (Shopfitting, 60 villagers: dummies in the window, bolts of cloth on a stand, shears on the bracket) → Draper & Outfitter → Drapery Emporium (plate glass up two floors, a flag on the ridge); a weatherboarded **Ironmonger** (Mills & Smithies: pails hung along the front, an open store at the side with ladders and timber, a key on the bracket) → Hardware Merchant (a mangle, a wheelbarrow, galvanised bins); a cream, hipped **Apothecary** (Medicine: bow windows with great coloured carboys that glow at night, a mortar and pestle) → Chemist & Druggist → Dispensing Chemist (Hospitals: a penny weighing machine outside); a **Stationer** under a half-hipped roof (Schooling: books in the window, the day's bills on a board, a red pillar box; its readers add 1–2 research points a day) → Bookseller & Library (an oriel window, a barrow of books); a **Coffee House** with bow windows (Hospitality) → Tea Rooms (painted, an awning and tables outside) → Grand Café (a glazed winter garden across the front, parasols and palms); a whitewashed **Post Office** with a red front, a clock and the postman's cart (Public Works) → General Post Office (Electricity: the clock in a pediment, a telegraph pole on the roof, the mail van outside); a stone **Savings Bank** with a columned porch (Grand Shops, 120 villagers: few visits, small fees, well-paid clerks with some schooling) → Town Bank (a portico and a balustrade, Civic Planning). **Offices** have no counter but pay well, for villagers with some schooling: a brick Georgian **Counting House** (Shopfitting) → **Chambers** (a gabled bay of arched windows) → a five-storey stone **Office Building** (Tall Buildings, with a lift house and a flag). Every shop sells a trade, and a villager out shopping goes for one the town has (everyday food most often, clothes, boots, hardware, remedies, books, the post and the bank less, and a trade sold only far from home less still), to one of the nearer shops selling it: a street of bakeries doesn't draw every errand, and the town's only draper still gets the clothes shopping. The building guide and the building panel say what each shop sells.
  - **From behind** each shop still shows its trade: the back door at one end, its name on a board across the back, loading doors up the far end with a hoist (a sack, a bolt of cloth or a crate on the rope) at the bigger shops, and the trade's own things by the door: crates of greens at the greengrocer's, the butcher's smoke-house flue and meat safe, the cobbler's lit workshop window and a hide drying on a frame, bales at the draper's, bar iron and drums of paraffin at the ironmonger's, the chemist's green dispensary window and the tin flue of its still, skylights in the stationer's roof, the coffee house's stovepipe and milk churns, the post office's red loading doors and mail sacks, the bank's barred windows and studded iron door, the bakery's bread oven built out from the wall, and a fire escape down the back of Chambers and the Office Building.
  - **On a street corner** (a side street running down one side of it) a shop carries its shopfront round the corner: windows with its goods, the fascia and its awning down the side street too, a pilaster where the two fronts meet and some of its goods on the side pavement. The bank shows its banking hall down the side street, a tavern its bar windows and a lantern on the corner, and a shop with homes above its end shop. The town works this out from the roads, so laying or taking up a side street changes it.
- **Play and games** — the Parks menu has places to play as well as to stroll. A **Swing Green** (from the start) grows into a **Playground** with a sandpit, a slide and a roundabout, then an **Adventure Playground** with a wooden fort; a **Bowling Green** (Gardening) becomes a **Croquet Lawn** with a pavilion, then **Tennis Courts** inside a wire fence; and on a 2×2 plot a **Playing Field** (Landscaping, 60 villagers) becomes a **Cricket Ground** with its pavilion, scoreboard and sightscreens, then a **Football Ground** with a covered grandstand, terracing and a turnstile gate (Railways, 200 villagers). Children head for somewhere to play after school, and homes with children are happier (+4) with a play area or playing field in reach. A play area joined with another, a games lawn or a green makes a **Recreation Ground** (a playground, a lawn with goalposts and a shelter), then a **People's Park** with a paddling pool and a drinking fountain.
- **Branching homes** — a Cottage becomes a Family House, which then goes one of two ways: a **grand home** (Villa → Manor House → Grand Manor: few, wealthy residents who pay much more tax) or **flats** (Townhouse Flats → Tenement → Mansion Block: many households on one plot). Pick a **style** for any home or shop upgrade — timber frame, limewash, painted, brick, stone or clapboard — and tick **Preview in town** to see it in place before building. Each style builds differently, not just in different colours (and within a style each building keeps its own plan): timber frame has steep roofs, a jettied upper floor and a central stack; limewash has thatch or half-hipped roofs, a chimney up the outside wall and a deep front garden; painted is a symmetrical Georgian front with a hipped roof, a centred door and a chimney at each end; brick has gables, bay windows, round-topped windows and paired stacks; stone has low slate or saltbox roofs, deep-set round-topped windows and an outside stack; clapboard has a saltbox roof, dormers, shutters and a deep porch. On a bigger plot a home can become a **Country Mansion** or a block of **Apartments**, and on a 2×2 estate a **Manor Estate** or a **Mansion Quadrangle**.
- **Construction** — nothing appears instantly. Builders from the **Builder's Yard** walk to each site and work round the scaffolding; a building goes up in stages (pegged-out plot and materials, foundations, walls rising, roof) and only progresses while someone is working on it. Hired hands from the village start every site straight away and keep at it until the town's builders arrive, so a job never waits for the walk out from the yard; with no builders in town, they do all of it. Each job takes a third of the work it first did (`BUILD_PACE` in [`src/defs.js`](src/defs.js)): a cottage goes up in under two game hours, and bigger buildings still need the builders. Tick **Priority** on a site's panel to send the builders there before anywhere else; a flag marks it until it's finished. A sandbox switch builds instantly.
- **Town hall** — five levels: Town Hall, Guild Hall, Grand Hall (Civic Planning), a stone **City Hall** with a clock-tower spire and a fountain in its forecourt (Municipal Government), and a domed **Civic Palace** with a colonnade and statues (Metropolitan Government). Each raises taxes across the whole town (up to +40%) and the research it puts in each day (3, 5, 8, 11, 14 points), and the last two make the streets around them nicer to live on. The models of the town hall and everything below are in [`src/render/civic.js`](src/render/civic.js).
  - **Its style:** a clock tower, a dome (Grand Architecture), Gothic (Dressed Stone) or a temple front (Architecture), at every level. The style decides what crowns it and how its front is dressed: a cupola or a flèche on the little Town Hall's ridge, then a dome on a ring of lit windows over a portico, a spired Gothic tower before the doors with pinnacles and dormer gables, or a temple front of columns under a balustrade of figures and at last a chariot. Its panel's **Its style** section has builders rebuild it in another for 20% of all the seat has cost, keeping its level. One with no style chosen has a clock tower, and a dome from the Civic Palace up, as it always had.
  - **Capitol and Parliament** (Capital City, after Metropolitan Government and Great Plots): the Civic Palace takes the land round one of its corners for 3×3 grounds (little parks in the way become part of them; it is never simply extended) and is rebuilt as the **Civic Centre** (wings either side of a forecourt with a fountain, lawns and clipped yews, flags, lamp standards, statues and railings) › **Capitol** (a storey taller, porticos on the wings, riders on the plinths, floodlights). The Capitol takes seven more plots for 4×4 grounds and becomes the **Parliament House** (two chambers with great windows, the central hall under its crown, a square with a processional way, fountains, statues and an avenue of limes) › **Houses of Parliament** (a tower at each end, more flags). +48% to +70% tax, 17–28 research a day, culture for the streets within 8–14 tiles; each grows only from its top level, and the grounds follow the seasons.
  - **Civic quarter** (Civic Pride): **Law Courts** (Assize Courts with Justice on the pediment › the Gothic Royal Courts of Justice, safety), a **Museum** (lions and banners at a temple front › a terracotta Museum & Art Gallery with twin towers, culture and research) and **Assembly Rooms** (a ballroom over a carriage porch › an Opera House with a gilded loggia and a copper dome, culture and evening visits), with the joined library: each kind with its middle within 10 tiles of the seat's adds 3% to every tax, on top of the seat's own bonus (+12% with all four). The seat's panel lists each kind and the building that counts for it; a civic building's panel says whether it is part of the quarter.
- **Research tree** — press `K` for 55 projects in 14 branches (building works, homes, stone, brick, colour, trade, leisure, industry, farming, water, power, public services, learning and transport). Each branch has levels: unlocking a building gives its first levels, and later projects unlock its higher upgrades and other paths. Capabilities are researched too: **Joining** neighbours, **Plot Surveying** (expanding and buying garden strips), **House Moving**, **Great Plots** (2×2 estates, and the buildings they open up), **Public Parks** (3×3 and 4×4 parks) and **Capital City** (the town hall grown into a Capitol and a Parliament). Click a project to see pictures of what it unlocks. Research points come from town halls, schools, colleges, chapels and your villagers (1 for every 60) each day, and later projects cost more, so a town works through the tree over many years. The top bar shows the current project's progress (or your banked points) and points per day, and the research window lists where each day's points come from.
- **Water and electricity** — a **Utilities** menu (`U`) with wells, water towers and river pumps (each draws water for a number of people), and power from water wheels, wind generators and coal power stations. Water flows through mains under the streets and power along them on poles and wires, to every road-connected building, so it doesn't matter where on the roads a well or generator stands; when demand outstrips supply, some buildings go dry or dark. Powered homes are happier, powered businesses earn more, and the grandest upgrades need electricity. With **Underground Cables** researched, the power lines can be buried, one street at a time from a road's panel or the whole town at once from a generator's: the poles and wires come down and the street looks the better for it.
- **What it takes to work** — buildings need more than a road. Street lamps are electric and go dark without power; homes without it fall back to dim candlelight. Powered machinery (manufactories, foundries, paper mills, timber yards, furniture works, waterworks and pumping stations, hospitals, university laboratories) stops or slows without power, and shops and inns past their first levels do little trade after dark without electric light. Bakeries, taverns and inns, paper mills, cider works, clinics and almshouses, fire stations and fountains need running water; homes without it can't upgrade, expand or grow their family. Services, schools and utilities only work as well as they are staffed: a clinic with nobody in it cares for nobody, and a school has as many places as its teachers can manage. Stopped works go dark, their chimneys cold and their wheels still; small badges over a building show everything it is missing, side by side (red for what stops it, amber for what holds it back), the building panel says what is missing and what to do, and the Issues panel groups them ("2 workshops stopped — no power"). Early buildings — cottages, wells, farms, woodcutters, fishing huts, builders — need none of this. The rules for each type are in `NEEDS` in [`src/defs.js`](src/defs.js).
- **Parks with character** — every park lays itself out from its own seed and keeps that layout as it is upgraded and across reloads, so two greens side by side never match: a great oak, a cherry, a pump, a maypole or a pair of yews in its corner; beds in quarters, long borders, a round bed with a sundial or a pergola walk; a pond, gazebo, fountain, glasshouse or tea kiosk at the top level. Everything planted follows the season (blossom, autumn colour, bare trees, bulbs, frozen ponds, a drained lido) and the lamps, bandstand lanterns and glasshouses light up at night. A park joined from a little one keeps it: a garden and a bandstand make a park round its bandstand. Visitors stroll between a park's walks and seats rather than into its pond. The models live in `src/render/parks.js`.
  - **New little parks:** a **Pond** (Duck Pond › Lily Pond), a **Rose Garden** (› Rose Arbour; much scenery close by), a **Bandstand** (› Band Pavilion; culture for the streets round it, no staff), **Allotments** (› Allotment Gardens; homes with elders in reach are happier, +3, and the old go and dig of a morning) and a **Herb Garden** (› Physic Garden; research, Botany). A **Zoo** on a 2×2 plot (Menagerie › Zoological Gardens, Zoology) charges at the gate.
  - **Joined and grown:** two ponds, or a pond and a garden, make a **Lake Garden** (› Boating Lake, with boats for hire); a herb garden with another or a garden or pond a **Botanic Garden** (› Glasshouse Garden, research). A little park can also take the empty plot beside it and be rebuilt as the double-plot park. On a 2×2 estate (Great Plots) a park can become a **Landscape Park** or a **Hedge Maze**, a lake garden a Landscape Park, a botanic garden a **Palm House** and a recreation ground a **Lido**; a park growing takes in the little parks in its way.
  - **Grounds:** with **Public Parks** a 2×2 park takes the land round one corner (5 plots) for 3×3 grounds and is laid out as a **Grand Park** (› Pleasure Grounds), and that again (7 plots) as a 4×4 **City Park** (› Central Park). The park it grew from stays in its corner, inside railings round the whole, with a keeper's lodge at the gate, a playground and garden rooms by its seed; the grounds keep its research or its gate. Saves, multiplayer and merging villages carry them whole.
  - **Plantings and monuments come in kinds.** A Flower Bed can be bedding, a cottage border, bulbs, street planters or wild flowers from the start, and a rockery, topiary or a rose arch (Gardening), a herb knot (Botany) or a specimen tree in a raised bed (Landscaping). A Monument can be a founder's statue, an obelisk, a market cross, a war memorial, an angel or an urn, and an equestrian statue or a column (Architecture), a figure fountain (Fountains), a clock tower (Civic Planning) or a triumphal arch (Grand Architecture). While you build one, a bar above the build menu picks the kind (or **By its seed**, so that each one picks for itself from the kinds that need no research); later, its panel reworks it as another kind for 40% of what it is worth, keeping its level. Both have three levels, each kind laid out more richly at each: a **Flower Bed** › **Ornamental Garden** (Gardening) › **Show Garden** (Landscaping, 25 villagers), up to carpet bedding, a pergola of roses, a rock garden with a cascade or four knots round a sundial; and a **Monument** on its plinth in the grass › a **Civic Monument** on paving inside railings, with beds and lamps (Architecture) › a **Grand Monument** on steps, a bigger work, floodlit at night and bringing culture to the streets within 6 tiles (Grand Architecture, 120 villagers). Each kind has its own seasons: tulips in spring and dahlias in autumn, a topiary peacock under snow, poppies on the war memorial. Kinds are a building's own (saved, sent to guests, carried through merges), and the wiki's *Plantings and monuments* page lists them all.
- **Expanding plots** — two steps. First **take the land** beside or behind a building: an empty plot ($60 of land, plus clearing any trees), a strip of a cottage's garden, or a small neighbour bought out at what its building is worth and demolished (it asks twice, and shows the plot it would take). Taking land is instant and the building stays as it is, exactly where it stood (the same spot, turn and floor height, even when the new land is beside it or on a slope), with the new land as its garden. Then, under **Upgrade**, choose what to build across the bigger plot: the bigger buildings it opens up (a Country Mansion or Apartments, a High School or a College), or **Extend** the building as it is to hold 50% more, for what that extra half is worth: a home, shop or service is built on to, with the house carrying on along the same front in the same walls, windows, eaves and roof (its ridge a little lower), or a back wing a storey lower behind it, making an L, or a U on a 2×2 estate, with no second door or chimney; works get a work shed and yard, utilities a tank or transformer house, parks more garden. With Great Plots researched, a double-plot building can take the two plots beside it for a 2×2 estate ($120 of land) and then be rebuilt as its estate counterpart, or extended. Land is only offered where the bigger plot opens up something bigger to build.
- **2×2 estates** — a double-plot building grown onto the two plots beside it can be rebuilt as its 2×2 counterpart, at the level it had reached, each with its own model over the whole plot: a Country Mansion becomes a **Manor Estate** (wings round a forecourt, a portico, formal gardens and a stable court; then the Ducal Seat, with an orangery), an Apartment Court a **Mansion Quadrangle** (four blocks of flats round a garden court), an Arcade a **Corn Exchange**, an iron-and-glass **Covered Market Hall** and a domed **Grand Galleria**, an Inn a **Coaching House** (a carriage arch through to a yard of stables and a coach house), a Joinery the **Timber Works** (a sawmill, drying sheds and a log yard; then Steam Sawmills with a tall stack), a Parish School a **Cathedral Close** (a cathedral with towers and, later, a spire, and its school round a green), an Almshouse an **Infirmary Court** (rooms and wards round a garden court behind a gatehouse), a High School or a College a **College Quad** (a gate tower, a chapel, a library and a great hall round a lawn) and a Town Park a **Landscape Park** (a lake, an avenue up to a temple, then a bandstand and a palm house). Each is a step up from its double-plot building grown to 2×2, priced in line with the rest (`node scripts/economy.js` sets them side by side), and the building guide and the research tree show them. Estates don't grow any further.
- **Demolition** — a crew pulls buildings down over time; the people inside move out first.
- **Restyling** — a home or shop can change style without being upgraded: its panel's **Restyle** section lists every style you have researched, and builders rebuild its fronts and roof in the one you pick, for 30% of what the building is worth (the same surcharge as changing style during an upgrade).
- **Joining and moving** — select a building next to another to **join** them on a double plot: two homes make a Terrace, a home and a shop a mixed-use **Shophouse** (shop below, flats above, selling the shop's trade and built in its style: the butcher's brick and white tiles, the draper's painted stucco and dormers, the ironmonger's weatherboards, the chemist's cream stucco and carboys, the stationer's half-hipped roof and skylights, the coffee house's pastel walls and bow windows, the post office's red front and clock, a pub front for a tavern, with the trade's goods in the windows, its sign and wares outside and its own back), two food shops (market, bakery, greengrocer, butcher) an Arcade, a tavern and a shop or coffee house an Inn, a chapel and a school a Parish School, a clinic and a chapel or home an Almshouse, a well or a pump and a garden or statue a Fountain Garden, two workshops or woodcutters a Joinery, and gardens, flowers and statues a Park. A villa takes in a villa or cottage next door, or a green or flower bed as its grounds, as a **Country Mansion**, and a block of flats takes in flats or a cottage as an **Apartment Court** (the grand home keeps its look). A middle school and a school or another middle school make a **High School**. Three joins make buildings of their own: a tavern and a workshop a **Brewhouse** (a brewing tower with a louvred ventilator, a copper house and a taproom; then a brick **Brewery** with a works stack and a horse dray), two watch posts a **Watch House** (a station with a blue lamp, a watch tower and a walled yard of cells, keeping a wider area safe than the two posts; then a **Police Station** with a clock tower and a police wagon), and a fishing hut and a market stall a **Fish Market** (slabs of the catch under an open roof, an ice house, nets and a boat; then a **Fish Hall** with arched openings under a glazed roof, and a smokehouse). Seven more make buildings of their own:
  - two herbalists, or a herbalist and an apothecary, make a **Cottage Hospital**: an entrance block with the red cross and a ward with a verandah, reaching much further. It becomes a three-storey **General Hospital** with wings and a horse ambulance.
  - two schoolhouses make a **Board School**: a tall brick range with girls' and boys' porches, a bell turret and a walled playground, with far more places. It becomes a three-storey **Large Board School**.
  - a chapel and the cottage next door make a **Chapel & Vicarage**: the chapel keeps its look, the minister's family lives beside it, and gravestones, a yew and a lych-gate fill the churchyard. It becomes a **Church & Rectory** with a clock and a spire.
  - a builder's yard and another, a woodcutter or an ironmonger make a **Timber & Building Yard**: more builders, working faster with their own timber shed. It becomes **Building Contractors** with a treadwheel crane.
  - a draper and a draper, market or cobbler next door make a **Department Store**: plate glass in every bay under striped awnings, showroom windows upstairs and a corner tower with a copper cupola, selling every trade but banking and the post. It becomes a **Grand Department Store** a storey taller under a mansard, with a domed entrance bay, a glazed canopy and its van at the kerb.
  - two offices, or offices and a bank, make an **Exchange**: a long stone front with a portico of six columns and a clock in the pediment. It becomes a **Stock Exchange** under a copper dome.
  - a cottage and a workshop make a **Craftsman's House**: the family over the workshop, with far less smoke. It becomes **Artisans' Row**, three houses with workshop windows and trade signs.
  - a coffee house and a village green or flower bed make a **Tea Garden**: the coffee house with a hedged lawn of tables under parasols, flower beds and lanterns, selling tea and lifting the street round it. It becomes a **Tea Pavilion** with a domed pavilion at the end of the lawn.
  - a stationer and a schoolhouse or chapel make a **Free Library**: a classical front with a portico and arched windows, and a reading room under a glazed roof, adding to research every day. It becomes a **Public Library** under a dome.

  A tavern, a watch post, a fishing hut, a herbalist, a schoolhouse, a chapel, a builder's yard, a workshop, a draper, offices, a coffee house or a stationer on a grown double plot can be rebuilt as these too, and a greengrocer or butcher as an Arcade. A joined building takes the higher of the two levels, but never one the town hasn't researched or grown into yet, and the join button warns when the new building has room for fewer people than live in the two. **Move** any building for a fee: builders take it down, a cart hauls the materials, and it goes back up on the new plot.
- **All paths** — every building's panel has a **Paths** button beside its type name (and the building guide an **All paths** link) that opens **Upgrades & joins**, a tech tree of everything it can become. Each row is a building with its levels as nodes, left to right, and a line leaves from the exact level a path opens at: another path after the top level, or a side path from one level. Rebuilds on a double plot or a 2×2 estate, the next stage of schooling and joins with a named neighbour, which any level can take, leave from the start of the row. Lines are coloured by kind and dashed while locked. Rows say how they are reached, the building's own upgrade paths sit right under it, where it stands now is highlighted, locked levels show what they wait for, and clicking a row or level opens its guide.
- **Waterfronts** — a Fishery builds a timber jetty out over the water beside it, and the Harbour a stone pier with bollards, a lamp at its head, boats moored along it and a crane on the quay. Both run out across the beach and the shallows until they stand in open water (up to about two tiles). They are drawn after a model is fitted to its plot, so they never shrink the building; anything else that leaves its plot is still pulled back in.
- **Hillsides** — on a slope a building stands on the highest ground under its walls (a little in from its plot's edges), so no wall is buried in the hillside, and never below the ground at its front door, so the door stays at street level. Where the hill falls away the rest is a lower floor in dressed stone with a string course, small windows that light up at night and a door on the side most open to the slope, like a house built into the hillside.
- **Streets** — buildings come forward into the verge of the street in front of them, while facing buildings and street corners stay clear of each other. Little paths run from each front door to the street, and villagers go in and out of every building through its front door: from the street to the edge of the plot straight out from the door, up to the doorstep and in (so a door set back behind a forecourt, a portico or a garden is walked up to square on, never across a wall), climbing the doorstep, the porch or the flight of steps in front of it as the model has them rather than walking through them. A door that doesn't face the street the building is reached from (down the side of a row on a deep plot, or round a corner) is reached from that street's edge of the plot, round the building and up between a portico's columns to it. On an open plot (a farmyard, a park, a market square, a yard) they find their way round what stands on it, the barn, the stalls, the statues and the pavilions, coming in from the gate and going out again (see [`src/sim/plotNav.js`](src/sim/plotNav.js)). Buildings drawn without a separate door (an engine bay, an open-fronted shed) have their way in marked; the open-air ones (a market stall, the Market Hall's arcade, the open fish market) are wandered about in like a park instead. A building with a street along its side or its back (on a corner, or backing onto a lane) never turns a blank wall to it: every window on that wall is there, on every floor, with a door on the ground floor (the side door nearest the front, the back door in the middle); shops on a corner carry their shopfront round as well. Yards, farms and towers show their yard or stand as they are.
- **Gardens and land** — cottages sit at the front of their plot with a real back garden (vegetables, washing lines, sheds, hens). Big homes can buy a strip of a neighbouring cottage's garden; their owners pay the cottage's owners, both plots change size together and nothing ever overlaps.
- **Farms** — a farm's barn, cart shed and yard stand at the front of its plot, by the road, with its home field behind. A farm can plough **farmland** beyond its plot: pick **Add fields** in its panel and drag a box over open land (in this fork the box is filled with squares of farmland at the farm's own angle). Fields go behind and beside the farm (never between it and the road), must touch it or its other fields, cost $40 each (plus clearing any trees), and a farm works up to 6, 12, 20 or 30 of them as it is upgraded. Each field gives work to about 0.4 more farmhands. The farm's own field behind its yard and the fields beyond are one farmland: the furrows and crop rows run on from one to the next, and a single post-and-rail fence goes round the outside, with a gate from the yard. Fields change with the seasons (green shoots, golden wheat, stubble, snow) and block building and roads. **Take fields out** (or the bulldozer) gives a quarter of the price back, and a farm that is demolished or moved loses them. A farm that becomes an **Orchard** keeps its fields, planted with rows of fruit trees (blossom in spring, fruit in summer and autumn), and works as many as the farm did. A farm also chooses its **produce**: mixed farming, wheat, barley, vegetables (more hands, a little less each), or livestock: **sheep**, **cattle** or **poultry**, which need far fewer hands but earn much more per worker. Livestock farms turn their fields to fenced pasture with a field shelter and trough, or a hen house, and their herds grow with the farm and its fields. A farm has no farmhouse to begin with. **Join** a cottage or family house beside it and it becomes a **Homestead**: a farmhouse goes up in the yard, the family moves in, and the farm keeps its level, fields and produce (Homestead → Farm & Farmhouse → Estate & Manor → Manor Farm).
- **Busy places** — parks, gardens and squares hold only so many visitors at once (more for bigger and upgraded ones; the park's panel shows how full it is). When one is full, villagers try the nearest park with room, or stay home. A park needs a road to it before anyone can visit.
- **Roads** — dirt paths, cobblestone and paved streets drawn as crisp surfaces with edging stones, plus wooden bridges over water with sloped approaches. Street lamps light up at night once the town has electricity. A road is drawn as a curve along the stroke of the road tool (`Shift` for a straight one), and the inside corners of its junctions are rounded with kerbs, whatever the angle the roads meet at; door paths and power poles follow along, and villagers keep to their side of it round every bend.
- **(Not in this fork) Diagonal streets** — in the main game, drag the road tool at about 45 degrees and the road runs **diagonally**, from tile centre to tile centre across the corner two tiles share, as far as the drag goes that way and then straight on along the grid (a drag nearer the grid turns one corner as before; `Tab` keeps the tool to the grid). A diagonal drawn into a street, or out of one, makes a junction there; it crosses streets and other diagonals in crossroads and turns back the other way or onto the grid in a curve, all rounded and kerbed, at any road level. It only brushes the corners of the tiles beside it, so it doesn't join a road it merely passes: draw a street from it into that road to join them. A one-tile building beside a diagonal turns 45 degrees on its plot to face it, with its door on the corner the street passes, fitted a little smaller between the street and the plots behind (and while it faces the diagonal it doesn't grow, join or deal in garden land). Nothing bigger may stand where a diagonal passes its corner, and a diagonal can't pass the corner of anything bigger, or of farm fields. Villagers, carts and coaches take a diagonal when it saves going round (a step across a corner counts about 1.4 tiles), and it carries the water mains and joins buildings to the town like any street. Bridges, boulevards and street dressings keep to the grid; a diagonal has its plain street lamps and power poles. How it works: one byte a tile (`world.diag`) holds the links across corners and the sides cut off from the roads a diagonal passes by; every tile a diagonal touches is laid from one distance field of discs, strips, the grid's fillets and the diagonals' capsules, blended where they meet at an angle, so the pieces meet exactly at the tile edges ([`src/world/diagonal.js`](src/world/diagonal.js), [`src/world/roadShape.js`](src/world/roadShape.js)). Diagonals are saved (only when a town has any, so older saves load as they were), sent to guests and turned with a merged village, and the way a building faces is worked out again from them.
- **Buildings at any angle** — in this fork a building stands at any angle anyway (see *This fork* above); what follows is how the main game turns one on its square plot. A building needn't stand square on its plot. While placing it, `Z` and `C` turn it in steps of 15° (5° with Shift) up to 45° either way from facing its street; one already standing can be turned, nudged across its plot or towards and away from the street, and squared up again from **On its plot** in its panel (the `pose` command). However it is turned or nudged it keeps to its plot and the verge it comes forward into: the model fit shrinks it as much as it must, so it never reaches into a neighbour or onto the road, and its door turns with it. A one-tile building facing the outside of a bend in the street turns by itself to sit square to the curve (worked out from the roads, like facing a diagonal). How it works ([`src/world/pose.js`](src/world/pose.js)): the town still keeps every building on its tile plot for occupancy, access, reach and coverage; `b.pose` (`{ a, x, z }`: the turn, and the nudge across and forward in its own frame) is saved and sent only when set, so older saves load square, and it turns with its town in a merge. Each built model's real footprint is kept as a polygon in world space (`b.foot`, and `buildModel(b).foot` headless) for checking what stands where.
- **Whole stretches of road at once** — **Select roads** in the Roads menu (or **Select stretch** in a road's panel) picks the run of road between two junctions with a click (in this fork, a stretch of the network: the curve between two junctions or ends), more runs with Shift-click, or every road in a dragged box or line (`Tab` switches). The panel shows what upgrading every tile one level, downgrading it or taking it all up costs or pays back (a quarter of the price comes back), what can't change and why (paved streets go no higher, the road out of the valley must stay), and who would be cut off: buildings left without a road or the way out of town, coach lines left without a route, buildings that step back from the street. Anything cut off takes a second click. Hovering an action shows on the map which tiles change and who loses out. `Backspace` steps back through your picks, right-click clears them; it works the same in the 2D map and at any rotation.
- **Dressing a street** — from the same panel, a picked stretch (priced by its length in units in this fork) can become a **tree-lined avenue** (street trees on both verges), a **hedged lane** (low hedges along both sides), a **lamp-lit street** (a lamp every other tile, lit at night when the town has power) or, on paved streets, a **planted median** (a planted strip down the middle). Each has a price per tile and a little upkeep, and makes the homes and shops beside it a little nicer to live by. Trees and hedges leave gaps at junctions, bends, driveways, lamps and poles, and take the verges: buildings that stood forward into the street step back behind them (the panel says how many). Swapping or removing a dressing, or taking up its road, pays back a quarter of its price; downgrading a street below paving takes its median off. The prices are in `DRESSINGS` in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js).
- **(Not in this fork) Boulevards** — in the main game, roads laid directly side by side become one **boulevard**, not a wide slab of road: a lane each way with a grass median between them, kerbed on both sides, with a band of setts along the median on cobbled and paved lanes. Where a street crosses or meets it the lanes join and the median stops in a rounded nose; at a dead end the lanes join in a turning loop round the nose; where one road carries on and the other stops, it merges into it; a two-lane boulevard turns a corner with its median; a level crossing breaks the median for the track; over water the lanes cross on twin bridges. Three or more roads side by side get a median between each. Boulevards come from the road layout alone ([`src/world/boulevard.js`](src/world/boulevard.js)), so they are never saved or sent: loads, guests and merged villages work them out again, and they change as roads are laid or taken up. A boulevard is a little nicer to live by than a plain street, and it can be given a **look**, chosen like a street's dressing (a **Boulevard look** section in the road selection panel, and buttons in a lane's own panel that dress the whole boulevard between its junctions): a **tree-lined median** (a tree on every tile, a lamp between every other pair), a **flower-bed median** (bedding for every season: bulbs, summer bedding, autumn colours, bare soil under the snow; needs Gardening), a **tram reservation** (paved, with tram rails, overhead wires on poles with lanterns, benches and now and then a shelter; paved boulevards, needs Railways), a **cobbled promenade** (a fountain, then a statue, every four tiles, with benches and lamps; the fountains freeze in winter; not on dirt, needs Fountains) or a **hedged median** (a clipped hedge with clipped balls either side of a gap to cross). Each look has its price, upkeep, unlock and appeal in `DRESSINGS`. A street dressing on a boulevard goes alongside its look and keeps to the lanes' outer verges (avenue trees along the verges and a promenade down the middle, say), as do their street lamps and power poles, and buildings fronting a boulevard come forward to the verge as on any street. A look goes again (a quarter of its price back) when its tile stops being a boulevard: the other road taken up (the selection panel warns), or a street laid across. Traffic keeps to its side: villagers and coaches go one way along each lane, keeping right, and cross the median only where that saves going round.
- **Transit** — horse-drawn **coach lines** loop between coach stops. Coaches and goods carts keep to their lane on the right of the road, clear of the pavements, follow its curve round a bend (steered by the horse in front, so the back cuts in a little rather than the nose swinging across the other lane) and keep their distance behind the one ahead. Each claims its whole way through a junction before it goes in, by the quarter tile (in this fork, in squares 0.15 across, counting the way it faces while still turning there), so ways that don't cross go through together; it never goes in without room to stand on the other side, and whoever has waited longest for a way across goes next, so a stream along a main road can't hold up those crossing it for ever. The rare few that end up each waiting on the next go on where only their turn at a junction holds them up, or else let the youngest of them back up a little. They stop for villagers in the way, and villagers wait at the kerb for one coming. **Railways** have track (with curves), stations and steam trains. Trains never reverse, so a rail line needs a loop of track through its stations; trains drive forward around it, ease in and out of stations, and stop centred on each platform (up to three trains per loop). **Gondolas** (the Cable Cars research, 40 villagers) need no road or track between their stations, and follow La Paz's **Mi Teleférico**: lines open in its colours and are named for them (the **Red Line** first, then Yellow, Green, Blue, Orange, White, Sky Blue, Purple, Brown and Silver), their cabins rounded, in the line's colour with a pale band at the sills, big windows between posts in the colour, a door, a fan cowl and a swept hanger to the grip about as long as the cabin is tall (`G_HANG`), so the cable passes over the roof however steeply it climbs (no span is steeper than one in one, `G_STEEP`: where one would be, its lower end rises; through a station it runs level, `G_LEVEL`), and lit inside at night with a lamp at each end, white ahead and red behind; the towers pale tapering steel tubes with a band of the line's colour, a crossarm and walkway just under the ropes, a train of sheaves under each and a red lamp on top. A gondola station is a concrete deck on piers under a broad modern roof (flat, a single pitch or a butterfly), with a glazed booking office below and stairs up from the street, the line's colour round its deck, over its door and on the white hood of the drive over its bullwheel (`b.tint`, from transit.js); a Gondola Terminal has a glazed hall and a wider roof. Stations are lit all night (with power): the office, the name board, strips of light under the roof, light on the deck and the roof's edge glowing in the line's colour. The stairs are to the villagers' scale (steps of 0.02 on treads of 0.03, a fifth of a grown-up's height) and wind round the outside of the deck in flights with a landing at each corner, on whichever side keeps them clear of the cabins (`b.cables`, the headings the cables leave on, worked out by transit.js and never saved). The model gives the way up and the deck (`b.stair`, `b.deck`): riders climb the stairs (or ride the glass lift of a tall station: its car, `agents.lifts`, waits with its doors open for those at its door, takes up to four up or down together, each at a spot of their own in a row along its long car (`LIFT`, in buildings.js), in sight through the glass, and comes empty to whoever waits at the other end) and wait on the deck, which is furnished for it (railings round its edge, open only where the cabins and the way up come through; benches along its quiet sides facing in, with arms and a glazed screen at the Terminal and a board of the times; flower boxes, lamps and a clock on the drive): they take a free seat (`b.seats`) or stand clear of the cabins' way (`b.stand`) facing where they come in, walk across to a cabin when it stands there (it waits for them) and ride in it, all six a cabin holds (two across and three deep) seen through its clear glass windows (`MAT.glass`); those getting off step out, cross the deck and go down to the street. Headless, without the model, riders wait and vanish at the street as before. Pick two or more stations in order and the cable runs straight from each to the next, over streets, roofs, fields, the river or a hill, out on one cable and back on the other alongside, round a bullwheel at each end station. Up to 12 cabins in the line's colour hang from it, evenly spaced. In a station a cabin lets go of the cable and creeps across the deck while riders get in and out; out on the cable it swings a little on its arm, and its windows light up at night. The towers carry the cable about every six tiles, wherever there is room along that stretch: open ground first, then a field or a park, then a road's verge (never on a building, in the middle of a road, on track or in water). Each stands just tall enough for the cabins to clear the roofs below it, up to six tiles. Where a tall neighbour stands close by, the station itself rises on longer piers, braced in steel (up to three and a half tiles more, with a glass lift beside the deck in place of the stairs) so the cable leaves it high enough. That height (`b.lift`) is worked out afresh from the town and never saved. Pylons and stations rise again when something taller is built under the line. A line whose cable still can't clear something is refused, saying what is in the way, and one that is later built under stops, shown as **Cable blocked**, until the way is clear ([`src/sim/transit.js`](src/sim/transit.js)). A new line is refused, too, if its cable would run alongside another line's, near enough for their cabins to meet, or cross one (or itself) with no room for a cabin to pass over (a cabin and its hanger), or pass over another line's station, or turn back on itself by more than a third of a turn at a station on the way, so no two cabins meet in the air; and where a line crosses an older line's cable, it passes well under it or over it, its cabins' floors clear of that cable, every time the routes are worked out (`_overCables`), as the town changes round them. A gondola station takes one line; where lines meet, a **Gondola Interchange** (2×2) is the station four times over, a deck with a berth in each quarter under one roof, a drive over each in its line's colour and a glazed booking hall below, and up to four lines call there, each at its own berth, the one facing most the way it goes on (`b.berths`, where each is and the headings its cables leave on, worked out by transit.js and never saved), so their cabins never cross on the deck. Riders wait by their own line's berth. `node scripts/collide.js` strings two gondola lines across every starter's busiest part and checks that no cabin or cable passes through anything. A station in the middle of a line is called at both ways, and riders take the cabin going their way. Villagers take whatever gets them there soonest ([`plan`](src/sim/transit.js)): the walk all the way along the roads, or a ride, counting the walks to the first stop and from the last, a gondola station's stairs, the wait (half the time between cabins, so a busy line is quicker to catch) and the ride; or two lines, changing where both call (a shared coach stop, or an interchange, stepping out of one cabin and across the deck to the other line's berth). Climbing counts on every walk: on foot each unit of height climbed takes as long as `CLIMB` (5) tiles along the flat (Naismith's rule, in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js)), so on a hill villagers really do walk slower, and coach and cart horses slow half as much; a gondola climbs for its riders. Each ride pays its fare. The lines window shows each line's trips this session and how much quicker than walking they were, those changing lines, and how far a gondola line climbs.
- **Animals** — a few deer and foxes roam the woods, rabbits the meadows, and mallards paddle on the lakes and river. Sheep, cows and chickens graze on the farms, out across their fields; how many depends on what the farm keeps, its level and how many fields it works. They wander slowly and unpredictably, check the ground a step ahead so they steer around buildings, rarely cross roads, keep to their habitat, and bolt from villagers who get too close. Animals are drawn life-size next to the villagers.
- **Villagers** — every villager is a little minion (our own take on the style), drawn to scale with the buildings (a grown-up comes up to most of a front door): a yellow capsule of a body in blue dungarees with a bib and straps, short stubby legs, bare yellow arms with black gloves, one big goggle or two on a strap round the head, and a little smile. Some are short and round and some tall and thin; children are small and round, and the old a paler yellow in faded dungarees. Some are bald, others have a little hair on top (a few sprouting hairs, a tuft, a combed cap, a mop or a single curl; mostly black or brown, grey for the old and now and then a bright dye). Work shows as small touches over the dungarees: a farmhand's neckerchief and straw hat, a builder's tool belt and hard hat, a shopkeeper's or a nurse's apron, a clerk's tie and bowler, a teacher's bow tie, a parson's collar, the watch's cross-belt and cap, and work gloves on some; the well-off wear a bow tie and a top hat or a bowler. They waddle on their short legs, rocking from side to side, with a soft shadow at their feet, and standing about they turn to look round. Builders swing their hammers round the scaffolding, farmhands hoe and orchard hands reach up for the fruit, shoppers carry home a basket or a parcel, children take a satchel to school, some of the old walk with a stick, a lantern lights the way at night and in winter most wear a knitted scarf and a bobble hat; children at play jump about with their arms in the air. Everyone keeps their own look for life (it follows their seed), changing clothes when they change jobs. Built from a few low-poly pieces and drawn instanced, only those in view, in fewer triangles and draw calls than the figures before them (see [`src/render/villagers.js`](src/render/villagers.js)). The panels show each villager as their own little minion, in their colours with their goggles, hair or hat and the touch of their trade, and the population and the Villagers directory have a pair of them for an icon ([`src/ui/minion.js`](src/ui/minion.js)). They walk at a slow stroll (half the pace they once had; the coach and cart horses walk at half pace too). Among those drawn they keep out of each other's way: a step to one side to pass (never far enough into the road to be in a coach's way), a queue behind someone slower or waiting, turns through a doorway (those going in wait for those coming out), and anyone standing about in the street shuffles over; strollers in a park pick somewhere to stand that nobody stands already. Every resident has a name (families share a surname, and names rarely repeat), an age, an education, a home, maybe a job, a wage, savings, living costs, a family, friends (workmates, neighbours, classmates) and one or two personality traits (early riser, night owl, homebody, sociable, big spender, thrifty, outdoorsy, devout, hard-working…) that shape their daily routine. Press `P` for the **Villagers** directory: search, filter and sort everyone in town, then click a name to follow them. A building with several households lists its residents family by family. Single-family homes take one family: a cottage, family house or villa holds one household (a Manor House, Grand Manor or Country Mansion two, a Stately Home three, the 2×2 estates four or five, a terrace one per house in the row), and newcomers to them come as one big family that all but fills the house, children, grown-up sons and daughters and grandparents among them. Since each newcomer household needs a home of its own, cottages (5) and family houses (00) are cheaper than they were and families take up empty homes sooner, so a town grows at about the pace it did when homes filled up with lodgers. The family make-up and arrival pace are `FAMILY_KIDS`, `FAMILY_KIDS_BIG`, `ARRIVE_RATE` and `ARRIVE_MAX` in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js). Flats, apartments and shophouses take any number of households. In a town from before this, a home with more households than it takes lets the newest move on to a home with room, a few a day, and now and then one moves away when the town has nowhere for it.
- **Ages and schooling** — a year passes every two days. Babies are born, children grow up, young adults move out of their parents' home, older villagers retire and eventually die. Children go to the school that fits their age (elementary, middle, high school) and some young adults go on to college or university. Each school has a fixed number of seats. Better-educated workers earn more, and skilled jobs need them. A school upgrades two ways: **larger** (more seats at the same stage) or the **next stage** (Middle School → High School → College → University), and the later stages need a bigger plot.
- **Happiness** — click the happiness figure in the top bar to see what feeds it across the town (water, health care, schooling, safety, culture, electricity, scenery, shops, somewhere for the children to play, transport, smoke, taxes, crowding, work and road access): how many points each adds now, how many the average villager could gain, how many homes miss out, and a button that takes you to the fix. A home's panel lists its own gaps first with what to do, and each villager's page shows their mood and what would make them happier.
- **Windows and issues** — drag any window by its title bar and keep several open at once; `Esc` closes the front one. Windows stay on screen on a small screen: one remembered from a bigger screen, or left open when the browser shrinks or a phone turns, comes back into view with its title bar showing, and one taller than the room left scrolls inside. On a phone, windows open in the middle at the full width, the building panel sits along the bottom and the toolbar scrolls sideways. The **Issues** panel in the lower left (`I`) lists the town's bottlenecks: homes out of reach of health care, schools or safety, school places short, power or water short, workplaces stopped or slowed for want of power, water or workers, sites waiting for builders, skilled jobs nobody can fill. Click an entry to jump to the buildings involved, one after another.
- **Economy** — money in dollars: taxes, shop visits, production, upkeep, a budget screen with charts, and RCI demand bars. New families arrive from the edge of the map when the village is attractive; unhappy households leave.
- **Time and seasons** — a day lasts 8 minutes at 1×; sunrise and sunset, lit windows, lamps, lantern-carrying villagers, fireflies, then spring blossom, autumn leaves and winter snow. The seasons turn gradually: each holds for the first half of its days, then shades into the next over the second half, the grass changing colour, the trees turning one by one, the wildflowers coming and going a few at a time and the crops ripening or going back to bare earth; snow settles over the first two days of winter and melts over the last day and a half.
- **Weather** — most days are fair, but now and then clouds roll in for a few hours and the heaviest spells bring rain (snow in winter), with a soft hiss of rain. In winter the snow lies on the roofs as well as the fields, and a fall leaves fresh snow on the roofs and a trodden layer on the streets that melts and wears away over the next few hours (the streets keep only a light dusting otherwise, so they stay easy to read). The weather follows the town's seed and clock, so everyone in a multiplayer town sees the same sky and the same snow.
- **Soundtrack** — a gentle folk soundtrack made up as it plays: a lute picking out chords, a flute or bells on top and a warm pad underneath. It slows into a minor key at night and plays quieter in the rain. It is synthesised in the browser, so there are no music files. Turn it off with the note button in the top bar, or set its volume in Settings.
- **Building guide** — hover over a card in the build menu for a summary of what the building does (cost, jobs, homes, reach, visitors, upkeep, what it grows into). Pick one and the Building guide window shows every level with its needs, and the other paths it can take.
- **Crowds** — every place villagers visit holds only so many at once: shops, pubs and theatres, chapels, clinics (villagers now go to see the doctor, the old most often) and parks. A full place sends people on to the nearest one of the same kind with room, or they stay home. Hover over a building or open it to see its visitors.
- **Speed and sandbox** — pause, or run at 1×, 2×, 4×, 8×, 16× or 32× (`[` and `]` step through them). Four sandbox switches, available for a new game or from Settings: unlimited money, unlock every building, upgrade without meeting needs, and instant construction.
- **Pop-ups** — money pop-ups, notices, hover tips, the name tag over a followed villager and the warning badges over buildings with no power, water or workers can each be switched off in Settings, or all at once with the bell button or `N`.
- **Hide the interface** — the eye button in the top bar (or **V**) hides every panel, button and pop-up to leave just the town, for a picture or to take it in. The camera, zoom, turning and game speed still work; clicks on the town do nothing. **V**, **Esc** or the faint *Show interface* button in the corner brings it all back.
- **Settings** — in group boxes, in two columns (one on a phone): Display (pixel-art look and pixel size, soft contact shadows, tilt-shift), Performance (presets and a row of buttons for each choice), Sound, Pop-ups, Game (autosave), and the town's Sandbox switches. Every change applies as it's made; Close just closes the window.
- **Always daytime** — Settings → Display → *Always daytime* keeps the light in the day: the sky shows the real light from 8:00 to 16:30 and then drifts gently back to the morning's light over the evening and night, never dark and never jumping. The clock, the villagers' days, the seasons and the weather run on as usual.
- **Tilt-shift** — Settings → Display → Tilt-shift (Subtle or Strong) makes the town look like a tabletop model: the middle of the screen stays sharp (always the middle, whatever is selected or wherever the camera goes), and the view goes soft by degrees out to every edge: a wide oval, the blur easing in with no line where it starts, growing faster up and down than across, and slower below the middle than above, so the sharp area reaches further down the screen. The colours get a little lift. The sharp middle can be narrow, normal or wide. It works in pixel-art mode (the blur keeps to the art's pixels) and in smooth mode, and is cheap: the blur is drawn at half resolution or less, about 0.05–0.1 ms of GPU time a frame at 1080p on a desktop card. It's off by default, and Battery saver turns it off.
- **Performance settings** — if the game runs slowly or keeps a laptop's fan going, Settings → Performance can cap the frame rate (60 or 30 a second instead of as fast as the screen refreshes), draw at 75% or 50% resolution (chunkier pixels in pixel-art mode), lower or turn off shadows, reduce or turn off smoke, dust, fireworks, snow and rain, thin out the crowds (the default: in a quiet street every villager is drawn, but where crowds gather only some of them, so a big town's streets look busy rather than packed), draw every villager however crowded, only about half of them or only those near the middle of the view, and draw just a few frames a second while the game is paused and nothing moves. **Battery saver** sets them all low in one click (and turns off ambient occlusion), **Best look** puts them back. They're saved with the other settings; the defaults look exactly as before.
- **Multiplayer** — host your town and send friends a short invite code; they join from their own browsers and build the same town with you, live. Everyone sees each other's cursors and what they are building. See [Multiplayer](#multiplayer).
- **Saves** — in this browser (autosaved every game day, every minute and a half, and when you switch away from or close the tab; when a different town takes the autosave, say when you start a new village, the last one is kept as **Previous town**), or as plain `.json` files. **Download save (.json)** sits at the top of the Save window (or press `Ctrl+Shift+S`), every saved slot has its own download button, and you can drop a `.json` save onto the game to load it.
- **Merge villages** — join two saved towns into one valley, from the Save window or the title menu. While playing, the map-pin button in the top bar merges a village into your town: a saved one, or a brand-new one (an empty valley or a starter town). You drag it into place right in the game view. See [Merging villages](#merging-villages).

## Multiplayer

Build one town together, live, with up to eight players.

**To host:** open the town you want to share and press `M` (or the people button in the top bar), then **Host this town**. On the title screen, **Multiplayer…** offers *New village and host* or *Continue and host*. You get a six-character invite code such as `K7Q-M2P`. Send it to your friends, or use **Copy invite link** to send a link that opens the game with the code filled in. Keep your tab open: your computer runs the town for everyone.

**To join:** open the game, choose **Multiplayer…** on the title screen (or press `M` in a town of your own, which is autosaved first), type your name and the code, and press **Join**. You wait on a *Waiting for the host to let you in…* screen until the host says yes; then the host's town loads and from then on follows the host's live. Everything you do (building, roads, track, terrain and trees, demolishing, upgrades with a chosen style, expanding, joining and moving buildings, research, taxes, transit lines, the town's name and the game speed) is sent to the host and applied for everyone. The sandbox switches stay with the host.

**Who's in is up to the host.** Each newcomer asks first: the host sees *Mallory wants to join* under the top bar, with **Let in** and **Turn away**. The Multiplayer window lists the players, with buttons to make one a visitor (who can look but not build) or remove them, and has the switches for asking, locking the town and what newcomers join as, plus **New code**. See [Safety](#safety).

The players box in the top bar shows who is connected. Each player's cursor appears in the others' towns as a ring in their colour with their name and what they are holding, and whatever building they have selected is outlined. If a guest leaves, everyone else carries on. If the host stops hosting or closes the tab, the guests are told and can keep playing the town on their own, save it in their browser or download it.

### How it works

- **Host-authoritative.** Only the host runs the simulation: the clock and speed, money, construction, villagers' homes, jobs and schools, arrivals and departures, transit and research. Guests don't run their own copy of the economy, which would drift apart (the simulation uses `Math.random` in many places).
- **One command dispatcher.** Every player action, in single-player too, is a small command object such as `{ t: 'place', type: 'house', x: 10, z: 12 }`, handled by [`src/commands.js`](src/commands.js). Single-player and the host apply it at once. A guest sends it to the host, which checks it with exactly the same `World` rules it uses for its own actions. A command carries only what the player chose, never options that bend the rules, and malformed ones are refused.
- **Snapshot, then deltas.** A guest joins with a snapshot of the town: an ordinary save plus each building's entrance and each villager's school place. Five times a second the host compares the town with what it last sent and sends only what changed: buildings and building sites, villagers, roads and how their streets are dressed, track, land heights, trees per tile, transit lines, money, research and the clock. Every 15 seconds it sends every building and villager in full (a keyframe), so nothing can drift for long. A ready-grown town of 200 buildings and 650 villagers costs the host about 0.6 ms per update and under 20 KB/s at 32× speed ([`src/net/sync.js`](src/net/sync.js)).
- **Villagers on guests are simulated locally, for looks.** Who lives and works where, and who arrives, leaves, is born or dies, comes from the host. A guest's own copy of the routines then walks them about, puts builders to work on the scaffolding and runs the coaches and trains, so its streets look alive without streaming hundreds of positions. Individual villagers may therefore be in a different spot on each screen. The money from shop visits and fares is always the host's ([`src/net/replica.js`](src/net/replica.js)).
- **A tab in the background.** A browser gives a background tab no animation frames, so a host whose tab is hidden keeps the town running for its guests in larger, undrawn steps. Browsers may slow such a tab down further after a while, so a host should keep the game in view.

### Safety

Every other player is treated as untrusted.

- **The host lets people in.** *Ask me before anyone joins* is on by default. A newcomer waits while the host gets a prompt (*Mallory wants to join · Let in / Turn away*) and sees nothing of the town until let in. If nobody answers within two minutes they're told so; at most four people can wait at once, and someone turned away can't ask again for 30 seconds. A connection that has been turned away is ignored from then on, even if it says hello again.
- **Lock and new code.** **Lock the town** turns away every newcomer, and anyone still waiting, without disturbing the players already in. **New code** swaps the invite code for a fresh one: the old code stops working at once (its PeerJS id leaves the signalling server), and everyone already in stays connected.
- **Removing a player.** **Remove** in the players list disconnects them with *The host removed you from the town*; like guests of a host who leaves, they can keep a copy of the town. For the rest of the session the same browser tab (a guest keeps one PeerJS id per tab, reloads included) and the same name (ignoring case and spacing) are turned away automatically, and the host is told. Someone determined could still come back from a new tab under another name, which is why asking stays on by default; a new code shuts them out unless someone passes it on. **Allow back** undoes a removal.
- **Builders and visitors.** Builders can do everything a guest can. Visitors can look around and point with their cursor, but the host refuses every command they send, with the reason, and their own game says why straight away. The host can switch any player either way and choose what newcomers join as. A player with no role at all is treated as a visitor.
- **Guests can only ask.** The host checks every guest command with the same World rules as its own; commands are looked up by their own names only (no `__proto__` or `constructor` tricks), the sandbox switches stay with the host, and a guest can send at most 15 actions and 25 cursor moves a second.
- **Size limits.** A guest's messages may be 256 KB at most and the host's 64 MB; anything announced as bigger is dropped rather than buffered, and a connection that doesn't introduce itself within 20 seconds is closed.
- **No markup gets through.** Names of players, villagers, towns and transit lines that arrive from another player, a loaded save or a merged village are stripped of markup and control characters and capped in length ([`src/util/text.js`](src/util/text.js)), colours must be plain `#rrggbb` values, and the UI escapes everything it shows as well.
- **Joining is always a click.** An invite link only fills in the code; you choose whether to join.
- **Your address.** As in any peer-to-peer game, players you connect with can see your internet (IP) address. Only share codes with people you trust.
- `scripts/net-check.js` plays a hostile guest and a hostile host (prototype keys, markup in names, floods, oversized messages) and checks that each is refused or cleaned. It also covers letting in, turning away, nobody answering, the waiting limit, removing (and the same tab or name coming back), locking, a new code and visitors' commands.

### Why PeerJS

The site is static (GitHub Pages), so players need a public way to find each other. Kinstead uses [PeerJS](https://peerjs.com/) 1.5.5, loaded from jsDelivr with a pinned version and a Subresource Integrity checksum, the first time someone hosts or joins. It was picked over Trystero and similar options for three reasons:

- Its free cloud signalling server lets the host claim a chosen peer id, so the invite code simply *is* the peer id: `K7Q-M2P` is the peer `hearthvale-K7QM2P` (the prefix keeps the game's old name).
- It reports an unknown id explicitly, so a wrong or expired code gets a clear "no town is open with that code" message rather than an empty room.
- It configures STUN and PeerJS's public TURN relay by default, so players behind awkward home networks can usually still connect.

The signalling server only introduces the browsers. The town itself travels directly between them over a WebRTC data channel, or through the TURN relay when no direct route exists. Problems get a plain message: the library couldn't load, the PeerJS server can't be reached, no town has that code, the connection couldn't be opened (a strict firewall), the town is full or locked, or the host turned you away or removed you.

## How the buildings are made

Everything is generated in code — there are no model files, textures or sprites. [`src/render/geo.js`](src/render/geo.js) builds low-poly shapes (boxes, bevelled boxes, roof prisms, cylinders) and merges them into one mesh per building with per-vertex colours and a glow channel for lit windows. [`src/render/buildings.js`](src/render/buildings.js) has a recipe per building type and level, and every building's random seed picks its materials, colours, roof, windows and yard; [`src/render/forms.js`](src/render/forms.js) gives it its form (the roofs, gables, dormers, porches, bays, turrets and the model's choice of plan its seed picks, the same at every level). Brick courses and roof tiles are drawn by a shader, and the pixel-art look comes from a low-resolution post-process pass.

## Merging villages

**Save → Merge villages…** (or **Merge villages…** on the title menu) joins two towns into a new one. While playing, the map-pin button in the top bar (**Merge a village into this town**) opens the same window with your town as town A. Neither save is changed.

1. **Pick town A and town B.** Each can be a save in this browser, the town you are playing, a `.json` save you upload or drop onto its column, or **a brand-new village**. A brand-new village is either an empty valley (pick its seed and size; it comes with its founding families, as in a new game) or one of the starter towns. Town A keeps its name, its place and its road out of the valley; town B joins it.
2. **Arrange them**, in the game view or on a map.
   - **Place it in the town** (when town A is the town you are playing) pauses the game and sets the incoming village down as a real diorama beside your town. Drag it with the mouse; the blue, orange and dashed outlines show your town, the village and the merged map. **R** turns it a quarter turn, and the panel's buttons set it beside any edge or centre it on your town. The panel also shows what will be settled. **Enter** merges, and **Esc** cancels and carries on with the game as it was. The camera still pans and turns as usual: right-drag, WASD or the arrow keys, Q / E, and T for the top-down view.
   - **Arrange on a map**: a 2D map shows both valleys. Use **Left / Right / Above / Below** to set B beside A (the map grows to fit), drag B anywhere to overlap it or leave a gap, and **Turn B 90°** in quarter turns. B snaps to A's edges and centre as you drag. The picture becomes the actual merged valley a moment after you stop, and the panel shows the merged map size and everything that will be settled. A merged map can be up to 96×96: two big valleys side by side lose some empty countryside from their outer edges, never buildings. An arrangement too wide for that is refused with a reason.
3. **Merge.** The merged town loads, and a report lists what was moved, refunded, changed or dropped. If you were playing a town, it is saved in this browser first. The merged town is autosaved straight away and can be downloaded as `.json`.

How the join is made:

- **Ground** — each town keeps the land around its own buildings and streets. Where the two maps overlap, the join runs halfway between them, and any part of the square map that neither town covers becomes new countryside with its own hills and woods. In a band a few tiles wide each side of every join (auto-sized, or set **Blend width**), the heights are re-solved so each side keeps its own slopes and bumps while the difference in level is spread smoothly across the band (a Poisson blend). Buildings in the band rise or sink as rigid blocks, so their ground keeps its shape. A last pass eases any step steeper than the steepest in either valley. Ground colour (grass, sand, rock, shore) follows the blended heights. **Rivers and lakes** that reach a join are carried across: a river meeting water on the other side within a few tiles is joined to it by a curving channel; otherwise it runs on through new countryside to the map edge, or ends in a pool before the other town. Carried-on rivers keep clear of streets, and a street flooded by the blend gets a low embankment.
- **Buildings** — where two plots overlap, the more valuable one stays. The other moves to the nearest free plot with a road (turning a double plot if needed), or is refunded at full value if none is near. Buildings left in water, on ground too steep, beside water no longer (fishery, pump, water wheel), off their railway track, or cut off from every road are handled the same way. There is one seat of government: the more valuable is kept, and the other becomes a **Plaza** of the same level (a Capitol a Grand Park and a Parliament a City Park, on the same grounds), and the difference in value is refunded. Half-finished demolitions and buildings being moved away are dropped. Land deals survive only between two plots that both stayed put.
- **Roads** — a building beats a road, so streets under the other town's buildings are removed, and a diagonal street of one town that would pass the corner of the other's bigger buildings stops being diagonal there (its tiles stay road). Every separate piece of road is then joined to the network that leads out of the valley along the cheapest buildable route (bridges cost more than land). Stray scraps with nothing on them are cleared, a piece boxed in by buildings is removed, and a building left with no street gets a short lane, or moves. If neither town's road out of the valley still reaches the map edge, a new one is laid.
- **Ids and people** — town B's buildings, villagers and transit lines get new ids, and every reference moves with them: homes, jobs, families, transit stops, land deals and building moves. Villagers whose home vanished move into homes with room, households together where possible. If there is no room at all, they move away. Villagers whose workplace vanished look for new work. Nobody shares a whole name: the usual rule for newcomers picks a fresh first name.
- **Transit** — stops that are gone are dropped from their lines, and a line left with fewer than two stops closes, refunding its vehicles. Clashing line names and colours are changed.
- **Town** — research is everything either town discovered. Money is the two treasuries added together, plus refunds. The day and season are the later town's. Daily history is added up day by day, and sandbox switches carry over if either town had them on.

- **Landing page** (`index.html`, the site root):

- **Wiki** (`wiki/`): one page that shows one wiki page at a time, addressed by the hash.
  - Addresses: `#taxes` is a page, `#taxes/who` a section, `#homes/villa` a building, `#homes/villa.1` one of its levels and `#research/carpentry` a research project. `#villa` or `#carpentry` on their own find their way too.
  - A search box covers pages, sections, buildings, every level and research projects (`/` jumps to it). On a phone the contents fold away.
  - **Prose** (`wiki/pages.js`) explains every mechanic. It's written as functions of the game's data: prices, upkeep and the tables of roads, dressings, produce, trades, joins, estates and utilities come from `src/defs.js` and `src/config.js`. Numbers that only live inside the game's code (happiness points, tax per worker, the join, move and restyle prices, the research the villagers add…) are in `wiki/facts.js`, each with the file and the pattern where the code has it.
  - **Generated pages** (`wiki/catalog.js`):
    - a page for each building menu, with every building and every level: cost, upkeep, homes and households, jobs and their schooling, places, reach, visitors, earnings, water, power, smoke, research points, fields, builder-hours, what each level needs (villagers, research, happiness, services, electricity), how to get it and where it can lead. The figures are the in-game building guide's (`src/ui/guide.js`);
    - the research tree, with what every project unlocks and leads to;
    - the starter towns, each grown in the browser by the game's own code and drawn as a map.
  - **Pictures** are drawn by the game's own thumbnail renderer (`src/ui/thumbs.js`, with three.js from the same importmap), one at a time as they scroll into view.

An invite link (`…/kinstead/?join=K7Q-M2P`) or a Continue link reaching the root goes straight on to `play/` with the same query and hash, before the landing page loads. Anything else gets the landing page, whose Play button is one click to the game. Invite links made now point at `play/`.
- **Service worker.** `sw.js` stays at the top of the site and every page registers it there (the game and the wiki as `../sw.js`), so its scope covers all three. Each visit asks the server for fresh copies of every file, the wiki's included (it reads the game's own `src/` files).

### Keeping the website in step with the game

The wiki reads the game, so the building pages, the research tree, the starter towns and most figures follow every change by themselves. Prose doesn't: a new mechanic wants a line in `wiki/pages.js`, and a headline feature a card on the landing page. `node scripts/site-check.js` (part of `npm run check`) fails when the wiki falls behind:

- a building, level, research project, research branch or starter town without its entry;
- a key in `src/ui/input.js` missing from the Controls page, or one listed that the game no longer has;
- a setting in the Settings window missing from the Settings page, or one that's gone;
- a happiness factor in `src/sim/sim.js` that the Happiness page doesn't list;
- a number in `wiki/facts.js` that no longer matches the game's code;
- a broken link inside the wiki or from the landing page into it, an icon a page shows but doesn't load, or a file a page loads that isn't there;
- the root no longer forwarding a query the game reads.

The landing page's pictures are taken in the game. `node scripts/site-shots.js` serves the site with a shots page at http://localhost:8121/shots, which sets up each picture in a real town (the starter towns, a merged valley), draws it with the game's renderer and saves it in `img/`; `/shots?only=hero,night` retakes some. Retake them when the game's look changes.

## Running locally

There is no build step: it's plain ES modules plus three.js from a CDN. It only needs to be served over HTTP, because modules don't load from `file://`.

On Windows, use the bundled PowerShell server (no Node or Python needed):

```powershell
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File serve.ps1
```

Then open http://localhost:8080 for the landing page, http://localhost:8080/play/ for the game and http://localhost:8080/wiki/ for the wiki. Any static server also works, for example `npx serve .` or `python -m http.server 8080`.

### Playtest

```bash
node scripts/playtest.js 50 48 2024 economy
```

A bot plays a fresh town for a number of days (arguments: days, map size, seed, mode; with `PLAYTEST_SAVE=town.json` set, the town at the end is written there as a save, to load into the game, e.g. for profiling), building, laying roads, researching, upgrading, joining, expanding, moving and demolishing, and ploughing fields and changing what farms produce (the busy modes also make boulevards, give them looks and take lanes up again, lay diagonal streets out of town with homes and shops facing them and take a tile up now and then, turn and nudge buildings on their plots, and build law courts, museums and opera houses near the town hall). Modes: `economy` (real money), `stress` (unlimited money, everything unlocked, lots of building every day), `chaos` (the same with instant construction, so far more upgrades, joins and moves) and `scale` (a big ready-grown town on an 80×80 map, to measure speed). Every day it audits the town (no overlapping buildings, no broken references, capacities respected, building sites always progressing, no builders idle while sites wait, every field belongs to a farm, lies on open ground and is counted, within the farm's limit, the boulevards match the roads and every look suits its tile, the diagonal streets are in order and every building where one passes its corner is a one-tile building facing it, there is one seat of government on grounds of its own size) and it prints anything wrong at the end, along with the day's budget, how many buildings are stopped or slowed and why, and how many double plots grew into 2×2 estates and were rebuilt as estate buildings (the busy modes grow an estate whenever one can). The bot researches water and power once the town grows, saves up for generators, puts wells by trades that need water, and builds homes rather than more of a workplace that already stands empty.

### Collision playtest

```bash
node scripts/collide.js all 2
```

Plays towns headless with their real building models standing in, each split back into its solid parts (walls, sheds, stalls, fences, statues, steps), and checks that nothing passes through anything: no villager walking, working, strolling or waiting inside a wall (but for the step through their own door), every way in to every door clear and up its steps, strollers and outdoor workers keeping out of what stands on their grounds, no model reaching out of its plot onto a road or into a neighbour's, and no coach, cart, train or gondola cabin or cable through a building. Each town gets two gondola lines first, strung over its roofs between stations near where many of its villagers live and work (one with a station risen tall enough for a lift, where one of the best is), and an interchange where two more meet. `all` is every starter but the Metropolis, plus the market town with curving streets laid out of it and one with every building turned on its plot as far as it goes; name `metropolis` (or any starter) to play it, and `scale` for a big town grown by the demo bot. `--probe=id,x,z` shows a building's solid parts at a point, its door and the way villagers walk in to it. It also counts, over every moment played, villagers walking into each other (on the street, and in doorways and on stairs where they take turns), vehicles running into each other and villagers inside a coach or a cart, among the crowd as the game draws it by default (`--everyone`: every villager drawn), and fails a town at more than 10 in every thousand moments on the street, 30 in doorways, 1 inside a vehicle, or two vehicles touching for more than a couple of moments. The gondola lines it strings are the best the game will let be built. `--seed=n` repeats a run exactly; `--verbose` adds what holds the traffic up and how long a step takes.

### Starter towns

```bash
node scripts/starters.js
```

Each starter town in [`src/world/starters.js`](src/world/starters.js) is a fixed map seed plus a short build script (streets, buildings, upgrades with styles, cobbles, a coach line, the research its founders bring and what it researches next). The town is **grown when you pick it**, in about 50 ms: every step goes through the normal world rules (it costs money and waits for the villagers and research it needs; buildings go up with instant construction), and between steps the real simulation runs a day at a time, so families move in, find jobs and school places, age, have children, pay taxes and research. A town a few weeks along has research behind it that a few weeks of play would not earn, so the points for its founders' projects are banked on the first day, and each of those projects is done as soon as it can be begun. The town's own research then carries on with the next projects on its list. The result is saved and loaded back through the normal save format, so a new game starts from exactly what a save of that town would load. The sim's dice are seeded, so a town comes out the same every time.

Growing the towns at runtime was chosen over shipping saved `.json` towns because a saved town is a snapshot of the rules at the time it was made: when buildings, capacities or the save format change, it goes stale and has to be regenerated by hand, while a grown town always follows the current rules. The test checks that this still works: it grows each town twice and compares them, fails if any script step could no longer be carried out, checks that research covers everything built and that homes are within capacity, then plays each town on for a few days with the full simulation, running the playtest audits every day, and round-trips a save. Add `--map` to print each town day by day and as a map, which helps when changing a build script.
### The cathedral city

The biggest starter isn't grown from a script but laid out whole by [`src/world/city.js`](src/world/city.js), in about 0.7 s, through the same World rules (with every unlock and research project already behind it):

- **Streets**: a grid round the town hall, paved avenues and cobbled side streets, three tiles apart one way and nine the other, so every lot fronts a street. Streets cut off by water or a steep bank are taken up again.
- **Core**: the town hall grown across a civic block (the street between two blocks left out for eight plots) into the domed Capitol, with law courts, a museum and assembly rooms beside it and monuments and flower beds in the rest of the block (its civic quarter), then a market hall, a cathedral close, the exchange, a department store, banks, offices, a library and a theatre.
- **High street and homes**: every trade round the middle; then every lot, nearest the middle first, gets a home while there are more jobs than grown-ups, else a place of work. Homes run from flats and shophouses in the centre to terraces, family houses and villas at the edge. About one lot in twelve is a garden, a court or a play area.
- **Works**: on the side the road leaves by, with a wind farm past them (turbines need no hands, so the power never waits on skilled workers). No home stands within reach of a works' smoke.
- **Services**: placed wherever a home would go without health, safety, culture, schooling, shops within 7, a stop within 4 or somewhere to play, from where everything stands (nobody works yet). Then school places for every child of each stage, coach lines through the stops, and power and water with a quarter and a tenth to spare.
- **Settling**: families move into every home (leaving a little room), and the Sim runs four days.

It starts at day 245 with about 3,085 villagers, almost all at work, happiness at the top, and no warnings that matter. [`scripts/starters.js`](scripts/starters.js) checks it like the others.

### The Metropolis

The most populous starter is the cathedral city's generator pushed as far as it goes (`dense` in [`src/world/city.js`](src/world/city.js)): every plot of the largest (80×80) valley, cross streets thirteen apart instead of nine, and every home a mansion block, grand apartments or mansion court (about 36 people a plot), filled, round the Gothic Houses of Parliament on 4×4 grounds in a civic block of its own. Places of work are the densest there are (offices, banks and exchanges for the schooled; market halls, department stores and shops for the rest), built only as far as needed for about four in five grown-ups to have a job. People never leave a town for want of work (only an unhappy home loses its families), so housing could go further still, but the town would then live on very little tax. Power stations and waterworks fill a strip along the edge the road comes in by. Families move in as each home goes up and schools follow the children, so school places keep up.

What sets the ceiling is the browser rather than the land: the save of a town this size is about 3.8 MB of JSON, more than browser storage can keep twice over (autosave keeps the town before it beside it). So saves kept in the browser are now packed (LZW, fifteen bits to a character, [`src/save/pack.js`](src/save/pack.js)) to about a third; older unpacked saves still load, and downloaded files stay plain JSON.

Laying it out takes a few seconds, so it is marked `heavy`: the new-village window and the wiki build it only when it's picked. Laying out the cathedral city is unchanged: coverage, smoke and the power and water sums are now kept as buildings go up instead of scanned for each lot, with the same results.

### People audit

```bash
node scripts/people.js
```

How many people live or work in each building is set by how big it is drawn. The audit builds every type at every level on its own plot, in a few variants, and measures the solid bodies it is made of: walls, wings and towers, not roofs, fences, signs or props. It then counts their floor area storey by storey, taking floors from the houses drawn and one storey per 0.29 of height for anything else, and prints people per tile of floor for each building.

Each use class is set against a home, which takes about 10.6 people per tile of floor:

| Use class | Target density | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Offices, banks and exchanges | 1.6× a home | Clerks sit closer than families live |
| Shops and market halls | 0.75× | The floors above are mostly stores |
| Clinics, hospitals and police | 0.75× | |
| Taverns, inns, coffee houses and the opera house | 0.45× | Most of the floor is patrons' rooms |
| The town hall (and the Capitol and Parliament it grows into), chapels, the library, the museum and the law courts | 0.3× | Mostly one big hall |

Several kinds have no floor target:
- **Farms, industry and utilities** work out in yards and around machinery the floor doesn't show.
- **Schools** set their staff by their seats.
- **Mixed buildings** (homes over a shop or workshop) go by both uses.
- **2×2 estates** follow the double-plot building they are rebuilt from, so rebuilding one still pays. Their grounds have people too: grooms, gardeners and porters.

The audit also exempts a few buildings whose trade happens outside a roof, or whose people go by something else:
- open stalls;
- the music hall's performers;
- a tea garden's tables on the lawn;
- a fire station's crews;
- the cottage, which keeps a family of three.

Anything far off its target is flagged. When the capacities were last calibrated, each level was set to its target unless it was already within a quarter of it. Each type still rises from level to level, and prices rose with the people so paybacks stayed where they were (check with `scripts/economy.js`). Service upkeep rose half as fast as the staff.

### Merge test

```bash
node scripts/merge-test.js
```

This merges grown towns in every kind of arrangement: beside on each side, turned 90°, 180° and 270°, overlapping at several offsets, right on top of each other, apart with a gap, a small valley inside a big one, two big ones trimmed to fit, a town with itself, a brand-new village beside one and a starter town overlapping one, and two valleys whose rivers meet at the join. After each merge it checks the town invariants the playtest uses (no overlapping plots, no broken references, capacities). It also checks that every id is unique, there is one town hall, there is one road network that reaches the map edge and every building, nothing stands in water or on a cliff, no step on the map is steeper than in either valley, streets weren't flooded, everyone has a home, and buildings and villagers add up with the report. Research, money and the calendar must be combined correctly, and the save must round-trip. Several merges then run the simulation for a day. Add `--write <dir>` to save the test towns and a merged one as `.json` files to try in the browser.
